Image analysis system based on artificial neural networks


Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) is one of the leading causes of preventable blindness worldwide. Early diagnosis is often challenging due to the subtle nature of retinal changes and the high demand for ophthalmologic evaluations. With advances in artificial intelligence, it is now possible to develop tools that assist healthcare professionals in detecting the disease more quickly and accurately—helping to scale up screening efforts and prioritize suspected cases.

This website presents some results of our PhD research, in which we evaluated multiple deep learning models for the classification of diabetic retinopathy. We used publicly available retinal image datasets, including the Asia Pacific Tele-Ophthalmology Society Blindness Detection dataset (APTOS), the Messidor-2 dataset, and the Indian Diabetic Retinopathy Image Dataset (IDRiD), to train and evaluate five different convolutional neural network models. These models were later combined into a single ensemble approach, achieving 98% accuracy for binary classification (normal vs. abnormal) and 87% accuracy for multi-class classification (mild, moderate, severe, or proliferative DR). A preliminary version of our solution is available below.

Throughout our research, the analysis of different datasets led us to hypothesize that genetic and demographic factors may influence the progression of diabetic retinopathy, potentially affecting the performance and generalizability of AI-based diagnostic models. In this context, the creation of a new dataset composed of retinal images from a specific geographic or ethnic population would represent a significant academic contribution—advancing our understanding of the disease and enabling the development of more accurate and locally adapted diagnostic tools.

Our approach leverages the combined strengths of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Transformer architectures—two of the most advanced techniques in artificial intelligence—to perform joint image classification. While CNNs are highly effective at capturing local spatial features in retinal images, Transformers contribute by modeling global context and long-range dependencies. By integrating these two paradigms, our model achieves a more comprehensive understanding of retinal structures, improving the accuracy and reliability of diabetic retinopathy classification.

Part of the findings from our research have already been peer-reviewed and published at the prestigious International Joint Conference on Neural Networks (IJCNN), one of the most recognized venues in the field of artificial intelligence. The publication reinforces the scientific relevance of our work and highlights the potential impact of our approach on the development of robust, generalizable diagnostic tools for diabetic retinopathy. The growing interest in our methodology further validates the importance of continued research and international collaboration in this domain.

Despite the promising results achieved, one of the main limitations of our current approach lies in the exclusive use of publicly available datasets, which may not fully capture the diversity of real-world populations or the genetic and demographic variations that influence disease progression. From both a medical and computational perspective, the development of a new, curated dataset—gathered from a well-defined geographic or ethnic population—would be a scientifically valuable step. It would not only enhance the understanding of diabetic retinopathy across different contexts but also enable the creation of more accurate, fair, and generalizable diagnostic models.
